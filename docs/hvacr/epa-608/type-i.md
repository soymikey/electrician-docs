# Type I (Pages 17–19)

**Type I:** Persons handling refrigerant during the maintenance, service, or repair of small appliances must be certified as either a Type I Technician or as a Universal Technician, with the exception of motor vehicle air conditioning (MVAC).

The EPA's definition of a small appliance includes; products manufactured, fully-charged, and hermetically sealed in a factory containing five pounds or less of refrigerant. Split-systems may not be serviced by Type I technicians. The sale of CFC, HCFC, HFC, and HFO refrigerants to service or install refrigeration and air-conditioning equipment is restricted to technicians who are EPA certified under the Clean Air Act Section 608.

MVAC-like systems do not meet the criteria for Type I appliance certification no matter the type or quantity of refrigerant. When retrofitting or converting a system to use a different refrigerant, only EPA SNAP-approved substitutes can be used. Remember, there is no such thing as a "drop-in" substitute; EPA does not approve any substitute refrigerants as direct drop-in replacements to service an R-22 or any other system.

## Recovery Equipment

Recovery equipment used during the maintenance, service, or repair of small appliances that contain a refrigerant must be certified by an EPA-approved equipment testing organization. The equipment used to recover refrigerant from small appliances must be able to recover 80% of the refrigerant with an inoperative compressor and 90% of the refrigerant with an operative compressor. A refrigerant scale or tank internal float device are acceptable methods for monitoring the fill level in a recovery tank does not exceed 80%.

Any person who opens an appliance containing a CFC, HCFC, or HFC for maintenance, service or repair must have at least one self-contained recovery machine available for use. The exception to this rule are persons working only on small appliances. Technicians who open or perform maintenance, service, or repair of Type I appliances containing a CFC, HCFC, or HFC, can use either self-contained (active) or passive recovery equipment. A passive, system-dependent, recovery process for small appliances can capture refrigerant in a non-pressurized container. System-dependent or passive recovery equipment used for an appliance containing HCFCs or HFCs is limited to a maximum charge of 15 pounds.

Refrigerant recovery devices used to recover CFCs, HCFCs, HFCs and HFOs can be either manually closed or automatically closed when disconnected to prevent loss of refrigerant. When attaching a gauge set to check system pressures, hoses with manual or self-sealing valves on the ends should be used to prevent overfilling refrigerant storage cylinders. A standard vacuum pump designed for system evacuation may be used to evacuate a recovery cylinder to be used as a recovery device. However it cannot be used in combination with a pressurized recovery cylinder or vessel for refrigerant recovery.

## Recovery Techniques

When servicing a small appliance found to be leaking, it is not mandatory to repair the leak, but it is recommended to repair or replace whenever possible. Before beginning a refrigerant recovery procedure, it is always necessary to know the type of refrigerant that is in the system to prevent the mixing of refrigerants in the recovery cylinder. It is unacceptable to mix any refrigerants in an appliance or recovery cylinder. When R-410A or any other refrigerant is recovered from an appliance, it should be recovered into a recovery vessel that is clearly marked as to its content, to ensure that mixing of refrigerants does not occur. R-744, or carbon dioxide (CO₂), is a very high-pressure refrigerant and generally does not need to be recovered.

If a technician suspects that the refrigerant in a recovery cylinder is contaminated, a pressure and temperature reading of the cylinder should be taken and compared to a temperature-pressure chart. If the temperature-pressure relationship does not match, it is likely the refrigerant is contaminated and the refrigerant should be sent to a certified reclamation facility.

Comparisons to a temperature-pressure chart are only valid to check for contaminated refrigerant if both the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant are stable and known. When checking for non-condensables inside a recovery cylinder containing one refrigerant, the technician must allow the temperature of the cylinder to stabilize to room temperature before taking a pressure reading for comparison using the temperature-pressure (T/P) chart.

The service aperture or process stub typically used on small appliances is a straight piece of tubing that is entered using a piercing access valve to add or remove refrigerant from the appliance, Figure 16. When installing any type of access fitting onto a sealed system, the fitting should be leak tested before proceeding with recovery, Figure 17. Because solderless type piercing valves tend to leak over time, they should not remain on the refrigeration system after completion of the repair.

Both low and high-side access valves should be installed when recovering refrigerant from a household refrigerator or any other appliance to improve the speed of recovery. To ensure that all refrigerant has been removed from a frost-free refrigerator or freezer, the defrost heater can be energized to increase the refrigerant's temperature and vaporize any liquid. An appliance with an operating compressor and completely restricted capillary tube or metering device requires only one access valve on the high side of the system. If the pressure in an appliance is 0 psig after installing and opening a piercing access valve, the recovery procedure cannot begin.

When using a self-contained (active) recovery device and there is a large volume of non-condensable gas in the refrigerant being recovered, excessive pressure can occur on the high side of the recovery machine and in the recovery cylinder. Non-condensable gas in the refrigerant can be checked for by using a temperature-pressure (T/P) chart. The approximate pressure of an R-134a cylinder that does not contain any non-condensable impurities and is stored in a room at 80°F is equal to a pressure of 87 psig. As long as there is liquid refrigerant present and no impurities in the cylinder, the pressure and temperature should match what is shown on the T/P chart.

During system recovery and/or repair, a pungent odor detected from the refrigerant normally indicates a compressor burn-out. The technician should watch for signs of oil contamination when recovering refrigerant from a system that has experienced a compressor burn-out as the system will have to be flushed if contaminants are present. When nitrogen is used to pressurize or blow debris out of the system, the nitrogen may be vented to the ambient air.

## Hydrocarbon (A3) Refrigerants

Many Type I small appliances may be charged with a hydrocarbon (HC) refrigerant. Under ASHRAE Standard 34, hydrocarbon (HC) refrigerants are classified as A3, highly flammable.

Hydrocarbon refrigerants are not approved for retrofit into existing household refrigerators and freezers. Newly manufactured household refrigerators and freezers may be factory charged with any of the following refrigerants: R-290, R-441A, R-600a. These refrigerants are EPA SNAP-approved and are restricted to a charge size of 150 g or less.

| Maximum Allowable Charge Size — Per UL 60335-2-24: 2017 (SNAP-Approved, EPA SNAP Ruling 22:2018) | |
| --- | --- |
| R-290, R-600a, R-441A | 150 g = 5.3 oz |

A3 refrigerants can only be used in new appliances specifically designed for them. These systems must be hermetically sealed and must have specified colored markings to indicate a flammable refrigerant charge. A3 refrigerants are not approved for split systems.

## Safety

Household appliances charged with hydrocarbon (HC) refrigerants require permanent safety markings on or near any evaporators, exposed refrigerant tubing, the machine compartment, and exterior of the refrigerator, Figure 18.

When recovering refrigerants, safe work practices include wearing safety eyewear and butyl-lined gloves when connecting/disconnecting hoses. When using nitrogen to service, leak test, or purge a refrigeration system, the nitrogen tank should be equipped with a regulator. Refrigerants should never be mixed in any appliance.

Because refrigerants are heavier than air and displace oxygen, a very large refrigerant leak can cause suffocation. If a large leak of refrigerant occurs, such as from a filled cylinder in an enclosed area, and no self-contained breathing apparatus is available, vacate and naturally ventilate the area. At high temperatures, CFC and HCFC refrigerants can decompose into phosgene gas, hydrochloric, and/or hydrofluoric acids.
