# Type III (Pages 25–27)

**Type III** — Technicians maintaining, servicing, repairing, or disposing of low-pressure appliances must be certified as a Type III Technician or a Universal Technician. Most low-pressure appliances are used for comfort cooling and are subject to a lower allowable leak rate.

## Leak Detection

Because a low-pressure system operates below atmospheric pressure (in a vacuum), leaks in the gaskets or fittings will cause air and moisture to enter the system. To prevent air accumulation into an idled low-pressure refrigeration system, maintain system pressure slightly above atmospheric pressure.

Low-pressure chillers require purge units because they operate below atmospheric pressure, Figure 20. The primary purpose of a purge unit is to remove non-condensables that leak into a low-pressure chiller system. The purge unit takes its suction from a connection on top of the condenser where most non-condensables accumulate. After separating the air from the refrigerant, the purge unit sends the refrigerant back to the evaporator. High head pressure indicates air in a low-pressure system and that the purge unit may not be operating properly. Excessive running of a purge system on a low-pressure chiller generally indicates a leaking system. Purge units expel the air that is separated from the refrigerant to the outdoors. Typically, there is a small percentage of refrigerant vapor that will be mixed with this air. High-efficiency purge units will discharge a lower percentage of refrigerant with the air they remove.

A hydrostatic tube test kit is used to determine if a tube is leaking. When leak testing a low-pressure centrifugal chiller with nitrogen, the maximum test pressure is 10 psig. Leak testing a low-pressure refrigeration system with nitrogen in excess of 10 psig could cause the rupture disc, which typically bursts at 15 psig, to fail. Charged low-pressure refrigerating machines may be most efficiently leak checked by adding heat with circulated hot water or heating blankets. This will raise the system pressure above 0 psig, allowing the leak to be found using a leak detector. To reduce refrigerant loss from the purge unit on a chiller that is charged with R-1233zd (or any other low-pressure refrigerant), always leak test and repair the chiller. A low-pressure refrigeration system could have a leak in the condenser or chiller barrel tubes if excessive moisture continuously collects in the purge unit.

Moisture most frequently enters the refrigerant system in low-pressure chillers through leaks in gaskets or fittings. To check for a refrigerant leak on the water side of a chiller, place a leak detector probe in the drain valve opening of the water box with the water removed. Drain the water sides of the evaporator and condenser prior to recovering refrigerant from a chiller suspected of having tube leaks. According to ASHRAE Guideline 3, the system should be checked for leaks if, during a standard vacuum test, the pressure in a system rises from 1 mm Hg to a level above 2.5 mm Hg.

## Leak Repair Requirements

EPA's leak repair requirements apply to all appliances using a regulated refrigerant with a charge size of 50 pounds or greater. Once the leak rate threshold has been exceeded, the owner must repair the appliance to bring the leak rate below the threshold, retrofit, or retire the appliance.

The applicable maximum leak rates are:

- **10%** of the charge per year when used for **comfort cooling**.
- **20%** of the charge per year when used for **commercial refrigeration**.
- **30%** of the charge per year when used for **industrial process refrigeration**.

Chillers generally belong to the EPA leak repair category for comfort cooling. When used in multiple categories, the applicable maximum leak rate is determined by the highest percentage of use, Figure 21.

**Example:** If a company diverts 55% of its chiller's cooling capacity to one of its manufacturing lines and 45% of its cooling capacity to cool the office spaces, the chiller is considered in the industrial process refrigeration (IPR) leak rate category.

Use the charge stated on the equipment nameplate to determine a chiller's normal charge for leak rate calculations. The leak rate of an appliance using a regulated refrigerant must be calculated when topping off or recharging a system due to a loss of charge.

If an appliance with 50 or more pounds of a regulated refrigerant charge has exceeded the threshold leak rate, the owner or operator has 18 months to retrofit or retire the appliance if the replacement appliance will use a refrigerant exempt from the venting prohibition. If an appliance containing 50 or more pounds of a regulated refrigerant has exceeded the threshold leak rate, the owner or operator has 30 days to repair the appliance so that it leaks below the threshold and conduct the initial verification test. If a chiller with a charge of 200 pounds of an ozone-depleting refrigerant has passed the initial verification leak test, a follow-up verification test must be conducted within 10 days. Not having a certified service technician available for service cannot be used as a reason to extend the appliance leak repair deadline. System mothballing does not require removing the appliance and storing it in a warehouse at the facility.

Technicians who maintain, service, repair or dispose of low-pressure appliances containing refrigerant, must provide records to the owners or operators of such appliances that include all but the full charge of the appliance and the leak rate method. The owner and/or operator of the equipment is responsible for and must keep records of all leak inspections, initial verification, and any completed leak repair follow-up verification tests for three (3) years.

## Recovery Techniques

The high-pressure cut-out control on a recovery unit used for recovering the refrigerant from a low-pressure chiller is typically set for 10 psig. Due to the volume of liquid refrigerant in large systems, refrigerant removal from a low-pressure system starts with liquid removal, followed by recovery of the vapor refrigerant. After all the liquid has been removed, an average 350-ton, R-123 chiller, at 0 psig, would still have approximately 100 pounds of refrigerant in the vapor state.

During evacuation of a system containing large amounts of moisture, it may be necessary to break the vacuum using nitrogen. Increasing the pressure with nitrogen can help increase the speed and quality of the evacuation. During refrigerant recovery, water is circulated through a chiller to prevent freezing of water in the appliance. The system's water pumps and recovery compressor should be on during vapor or liquid removal from a low-pressure refrigeration system. Raising the temperature of the system or the room in which a low-pressure chiller is located will also result in faster refrigerant recovery.

When removing refrigerant oil, it should be heated to 130°F as less refrigerant will be contained in the oil at the higher temperature. Be careful when heating the system, since the rupture disc on a recovery vessel for low-pressure refrigerants relieves at 15 psig, Figure 22.

## Recovery Requirements

After reaching the required recovery evacuation level on a low-pressure appliance, wait a few minutes to see if the system pressure rises before charging. When leaks in the appliance make recovery to the prescribed level unattainable, do not proceed with the recovery. Before disposing of a low-pressure appliance, the refrigerant must be recovered or evacuated to 25 mm of Hg absolute. Technicians must maintain/keep recovery records for disposed appliances with 5–50 lbs. of refrigerant for three (3) years.

Replacing or repairing a compressor, condenser, or evaporator coil is considered a major repair. When making a non-major repair, pressurize a charged system by warming the liquid refrigerant in the system with hot water or warming blankets which will bring the system pressure out of a vacuum.

**Note:** Depending on the question, the EPA exam may use the word EVACUATION LEVEL to refer to the target level that a system must be recovered down to in order to meet federal requirements. When referring to recovery evacuation levels, the pressure will be listed in psig or inches/millimeters of mercury (Hg). When referring to a system evacuation level with a vacuum pump, the pressure will be listed in microns.

## Recharging Techniques

Before refrigerant recovery or system evacuation, an oil sample should be taken if the unit has had a compressor burn-out. Also, when pulling a vacuum on a system that contains a large amount of moisture, triple evacuate the system using dry nitrogen to break the vacuum. Increasing the pressure with nitrogen will prevent the moisture from freezing.

When charging refrigerant into systems with a water circuit that are under a vacuum, vapor refrigerant should be charged into the system until the refrigerant saturation temperature is above freezing (about 40°F). This will prevent liquid refrigerant charged into the system from freezing water in the heat exchanger tube bundle. To determine when vapor has entered the refrigeration system before liquid charging can begin, wait until the saturation temperature increases above 32°F to prevent freezing of water in the tubes. For example: charging liquid R-1233zd into a low-pressure refrigeration system in a vacuum greater than 14 inches Hg vacuum will cause the system water to freeze. At approximately 13 inches Hg vacuum, R-1233zd has a saturation temperature of 40°F. Liquid charging can begin in a water-cooled chiller with R-1233zd after vapor charging has decreased and when system pressures reach a minimum of 14 inches Hg vacuum. The liquid refrigerant is charged into the system using the evaporator charging valve, which is the lowest access point on a low-pressure appliance.

## Safety

When the TLV-TWA (Threshold Limit Value – Time Weighted Average) is exceeded, ASHRAE Standard 15 requires that each machinery room activate an alarm and a mechanical ventilation system. ASHRAE Standard 15 also requires the use of room sensors and alarms to detect refrigerant leaks in all refrigerant safety groups, Figure 23.

R-123 refrigerant is classified as B1 (non-flammable, high toxicity) and refrigerant R-1233zd is classified A1 (non-flammable, low-toxicity) under Standard 34. The use of room sensors and alarms to detect refrigerant leaks in all refrigerant safety groups is required not only because of refrigerant characteristics, but because refrigerants are heavier than air and can displace oxygen.

The rupture disc on a centrifugal chiller, located on the evaporator, opens at 15 psig. The discharge from a rupture disc or pressure relief valves should be piped outdoors to prevent venting into the machine room. All pressure relief valves should be piped or installed in parallel, never in series. When charging, refrigerant is introduced through the evaporator charging valve, make sure not to over-pressurize the system. Gloves and safety goggles should be worn when working with liquid refrigerant for low-pressure systems.

Figure 23 is a caution sign: nitrogen and positive-pressure gases; no smoking or open flame; the room may have insufficient oxygen; open the door and ventilate before entering.
