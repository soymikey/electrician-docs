# Module 00109: Introduction to Material Handling

This page organizes Module 00109 into 30 active-recall cards. Answer each **Front** question before expanding the **Back** to check your response.

:::danger[Safety boundary]
These study cards do not replace employer procedures, equipment ratings, manufacturer instructions, hands-on training, or qualified supervision. Never operate material-handling equipment or use a knot for a critical load unless you are trained and authorized for that task.
:::

:::info[How to review]
Mark a card **Know** if you can explain the hazard, equipment limit, and required action without looking. Mark it **Unsure** if you remember only part of the answer, and **Do not know** if you cannot answer. Repeat the **Unsure** and **Do not know** cards first.
:::

:::

## Section 1: Material Handling

### 1.0.0 Material Handling

**Front:** Why must routine material handling be treated as a planned safety task?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Lifting, stacking, transporting, and unloading materials are common sources of back injuries, struck-by hazards, pinches, falls, and uncontrolled movement. Plan the task, select suitable PPE and equipment, understand the load, and protect coworkers before movement begins.

</details>

### 1.1.1 Pre-Task Planning

**Front:** What should you evaluate before manually moving a load?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Check the load's weight, size, shape, grip points, sharp edges, labels, and stability. Decide whether help or equipment is needed, identify the destination, inspect the travel path, and remove or avoid slip, trip, and fall hazards.

</details>

### 1.1.2 Personal Protective Equipment

**Front:** What clothing and PPE concerns apply to material handling?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Secure loose clothing and long hair, remove jewelry, and wear task-appropriate footwear and protection. Gloves should fit snugly and match the hazard, but they must be removed where rotating or exposed moving parts could catch them. Follow employer and site policy.

</details>

### 1.1.3 Proper Lifting and Lowering Procedures

**Front:** What body position and movements reduce risk while lifting or lowering a load?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Establish firm footing, bend the knees, grip securely, lift with the legs, keep the back straight and head up, and keep the load close to your body. Do not twist under load; stand upright and pivot with the feet. Lower with the legs and keep fingers clear of pinch points.

</details>

### Preventing Uncontrolled Movement

**Front:** When should a worker get assistance instead of handling a load alone?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Get help when the load is too heavy, long, awkward, unstable, hard to grasp, affected by wind, or beyond your physical ability. Secure loose or flexible pieces, control the travel path, and make sure another worker is looking and ready before passing material.

</details>

### 1.2.1 Stacking and Storing Materials

**Front:** What principles keep stored materials stable and accessible?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Keep aisles clear, use level support, observe marked stack limits, protect moisture-sensitive materials, remove protruding nails, and chock items that can roll. Step back or cross-key bagged materials as required, taper brick and block stacks, secure wind-sensitive sheets, and isolate flammables from ignition.

</details>

### 1.2.2 Working from Heights

**Front:** How should tools and materials be moved to or from an elevated work area?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Use the required fall protection and an approved rope, bucket, or lifting method rather than carrying items by hand on a ladder or throwing them. Keep both hands free while climbing, secure material after lifting, control the area below as a fall zone, and do not store material on scaffolds or busy runways unless the system is designed and authorized for it.

</details>

### 1.3.0 Knots and Hitches

**Front:** What is the difference between a knot and a hitch, and why does correct selection matter?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A knot generally joins rope or forms a lasting structure that must be untied, while a hitch fastens rope to an object and is intended to release more easily. Each has different load behavior and failure modes, so only a trained worker should select and verify one for the actual task.

</details>

### 1.3.1 The Square Knot

**Front:** What is a square knot used for, and what is its major limitation?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A square knot joins rope ends of similar diameter or secures a light parcel in low-strain applications. It can capsize or slip, especially when tied incorrectly as a granny knot, and must never be used for a critical load.

</details>

### 1.3.2 The Bowline

**Front:** What are the useful properties and limitations of a bowline?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A bowline forms a fixed loop that normally does not slip or jam and can be untied after the load is removed. It reduces rope strength, requires a sufficiently long tail and any required backup, and cannot be tied or released while the standing end is loaded.

</details>

### 1.3.3 The Half Hitch

**Front:** How is a half hitch commonly used?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A half hitch is commonly used as part of another knot or to add security and help control long objects during handling. A single half hitch is not normally treated as a complete secure attachment for a critical load.

</details>

### 1.3.4 The Clove Hitch

**Front:** When is a clove hitch appropriate, and what limitation must be remembered?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A clove hitch temporarily fastens a rope to a post, pipe, or similar object and consists of two opposing half hitches. It may slip or bind as conditions change, so its suitability and any backup must be determined by the trained person controlling the task.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Loose gloves reduce **grip strength** and can be caught by moving parts. Keep a lifted load **close to your body**, and treat the area below a lowered load as a **fall zone**. Hoist tools to an elevated worker with an approved rope and bucket. A nonpermanent rope fastening is generally called a **hitch**, and use a spotter when a load blocks your view.
:::

## Section 2: Material Handling Equipment

### 2.0.0 Material Handling Equipment

**Front:** What determines whether material-handling equipment is suitable for a task?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Match the device to the load's weight, size, shape, center of gravity, and destination as well as the route, surface, slope, clearance, and site hazards. Use equipment only within its intended purpose, capacity, and manufacturer instructions.

</details>

### 2.1.0 Non-Motorized Material Handling Equipment

**Front:** What general checks apply before using a non-motorized handling device?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Inspect the frame, handles, wheels, tires, axles, fasteners, restraints, and rated capacity. Balance and secure the load, keep the route clear, use enough people or a spotter, and keep hands and feet away from pinch and crush points.

</details>

### 2.1.1 Material Carts

**Front:** How should a material cart be loaded and moved safely?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Keep the load within capacity, balanced, stable, and low enough to preserve visibility. Secure loose material, push or pull only as the device is designed, control speed on slopes, and keep people clear of the cart's travel and stopping path.

</details>

### 2.1.2 Hand Trucks

**Front:** What should be inspected and controlled when using a hand truck?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Check the framework for damage such as stress fractures, then inspect handles, wheels, axles, toe plate, and restraints. Center and secure the load with the approved strap or cord, maintain visibility, and use help on stairs, slopes, or with oversized loads.

</details>

### 2.1.3 Cylinder Carts

**Front:** Why must compressed-gas cylinders be moved on a proper cylinder cart?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** The cart supports the cylinder upright and provides an approved chain or restraint to prevent falling and valve damage. Install protective caps when required, keep cylinders secured, and never drag, roll, or lift them by the valve or cap.

</details>

### 2.1.4 Drum Dollies and Carts

**Front:** What hazards must be controlled while moving a drum?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Confirm the drum contents, condition, closure, weight, and required PPE before selecting the proper dolly or cart. Secure the drum and control its center of gravity; do not improvise rolling or tipping methods that expose workers to leaks, pinches, or crushing.

</details>

### 2.1.5 Roller Skids

**Front:** How are roller skids used, and why do they require coordinated control?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Roller skids use steel rollers to move very heavy equipment across a prepared surface. The load must be properly raised, supported, balanced, and guided, and multiple workers may be required to coordinate movement while staying clear of pinch and crush zones.

</details>

### 2.1.6 Wheelbarrows

**Front:** What practices reduce the chance of losing control of a wheelbarrow?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Inspect the wheel, handles, tray, and fasteners; distribute the load evenly and do not overload it. Use a stable route, control speed, watch footing and slopes, and lift with the legs rather than suddenly pulling with the back.

</details>

### 2.1.7 Pipe Mule

**Front:** What is a pipe mule designed to do?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A pipe mule supports and transports pipe while reducing manual carrying. Use it within capacity, balance and restrain the pipe, keep the path clear, and prevent the load from rolling, shifting, or striking workers during turns.

</details>

### 2.1.8 Pipe Transport

**Front:** Why does a pipe transport require two-person coordination?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Long pipe can swing, roll, obstruct vision, and strike people or structures. The operators must communicate, lift and turn together, control both ends, inspect the route, and maintain clearance from electrical lines and other hazards.

</details>

### 2.2.0 Motorized Material Handling Equipment

**Front:** What must be confirmed before operating motorized material-handling equipment?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Confirm training and authorization, the machine's capacity and intended use, the load and fall zones, the planned route, surface and grade limits, pedestrian controls, and the meaning of all site signals. Follow the manufacturer's operating and shutdown procedures.

</details>

### Capacity, Nameplates, and Inspection

**Front:** Why are the equipment nameplate and pre-use inspection essential?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** The nameplate identifies capacity, equipment weight, and operating information needed to prevent overload and instability. Inspect safety devices, controls, brakes, tires, steering, alarms, fluids, restraints, and structural parts; report defects and remove unsafe equipment from service.

</details>

### 2.2.1 Powered Wheelbarrow

**Front:** What controls are important when using a powered wheelbarrow or power buggy?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Stay within weight and height limits, keep riders off, use safe grades, maintain control and visibility, avoid tip conditions, and secure the machine when finished. Follow all labels and the manufacturer's starting, travel, dumping, and parking procedures.

</details>

### 2.2.2 Concrete Mule

**Front:** What should be checked before operating or refueling a concrete mule?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Inspect tires, hinges, joints, throttle cables, and steering, and use the machine only for designated materials. Turn off and cool the engine before refueling. Use a stop board or chocks near an open pit, check the turning radius, look behind before reversing, and allow no riders.

</details>

### 2.2.3 Freight Elevator

**Front:** What special hazard is associated with freight-elevator doors?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** The upper and lower metal doors may close together without passenger-elevator safety bumpers. Keep hands, arms, fingers, tool belts, harnesses, and materials clear, secure the load, and operate the elevator only under site procedures and capacity limits.

</details>

### 2.2.4 Industrial Forklift

**Front:** What are the central safety rules for industrial-forklift work?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Only a trained and certified operator may operate the forklift. Inspect it before use, wear the seat belt and required PPE, stay within the load chart and capacity, carry loads low, drive slowly, protect pedestrians, avoid blind spots and pinch points, and never modify the machine without approved engineering or manufacturer consent.

</details>

### 2.2.5 Rough Terrain Forklift

**Front:** How does rough terrain increase forklift tip-over risk?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Uneven ground and slopes shift the machine and load center of gravity. Stay within load-chart and grade limits, level and stabilize before placing loads, carry the load low, move carefully on slopes, and retract the boom and lower the forks before travel.

</details>

### 2.2.6 Hand Signals

**Front:** What must the operator and signal person establish before using hand signals?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** They must agree on the signals and exact meanings before the task because no single set is universal. Maintain eye contact, use the employer's approved signals, stop when communication is lost or misunderstood, and know the signal that pauses all movement.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Hand-truck loads are often secured with **ratchet straps**. Before refueling a concrete mule, turn the engine off and let it cool. Inspect a hand-truck frame for **stress fractures**. Roller skids use **steel** rollers, pipe transports require two operators, and equipment must never exceed its rated capacity. An industrial forklift can create a rear-swing **pinch point**, and workers should use agreed hand signals when noise prevents clear speech.
:::
