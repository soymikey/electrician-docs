# 00101 — Basic Safety

:::danger[Safety boundary]
These notes support study only. They do not replace employer procedures, OSHA requirements, site-specific training, PPE selection, equipment instructions, emergency services, or direction from a competent or qualified person. Stop work and ask for supervision whenever a hazard or procedure is unclear.

## Quick review

### Verified module summary

Basic Safety develops hazard recognition before work begins and covers incident causes, fall protection, struck-by and caught-in-between hazards, hazardous energy, PPE, respiratory protection, environmental exposure, hot work, fire, evacuation, and confined spaces.

**Primary sources:** [NCCER Core Sixth Edition lesson plans](https://www.nccer.org/media/2023/03/Core-6th-Edition.pdf), [NCCER Core curriculum](https://www.nccer.org/craft-catalog/core/), and [OSHA Construction Focus Four](https://www.osha.gov/training/outreach/construction/focus-four)

## How to use these notes

- Read the **Online-source summary**, then explain the hazard without looking.
- Use the page number to find NCCER's diagrams, examples, and exact wording.
- Treat emergency response, PPE, fall protection, electrical work, respirators, and confined-space entry as trained procedures, not do-it-yourself instructions.
- Record the controlling employer procedure beside each topic during formal training.

#### 1.1.1 Incident and Accident Categories _(pp. 34–35)_

**Online-source summary:** Injuries, illnesses, property damage, environmental releases, and near-misses are different outcomes, but all can reveal uncontrolled hazards. Near-misses should be investigated before the same conditions produce harm.

#### 1.2.1 Failure to Communicate _(p. 38)_

**Online-source summary:** Missing, unclear, or unconfirmed information can leave workers unaware of hazards or changes. Use the site's communication method, confirm critical instructions, and speak up when something is not understood.

**Active recall:** What should you do when a safety instruction is unclear?

#### 1.2.2 At-Risk Work Habits _(p. 38)_

**Online-source summary:** Repeating shortcuts can make unsafe behavior feel normal even though the hazard remains. Pause, follow the approved procedure, and correct the condition instead of relying on past luck.

**Active recall:** Why does “nothing happened last time” fail as a safety argument?

#### 1.2.3 Alcohol and Drug Abuse _(pp. 38–40)_

**Online-source summary:** Impairment can reduce judgment, coordination, reaction time, and communication. A worker who may be impaired should not perform safety-sensitive work and must follow employer policy.

**Active recall:** Which abilities needed for construction work can impairment affect?

#### 1.2.4 Lack of Skill _(p. 40)_

**Online-source summary:** Training, authorization, and demonstrated competence must match the task. Do not operate equipment or perform specialized work merely because the task looks familiar.

**Active recall:** What should you verify before accepting an unfamiliar task?

#### 1.2.5 Intentional Acts _(p. 41)_

**Online-source summary:** Horseplay, bypassing controls, damaging safeguards, and deliberate rule violations create foreseeable risk. Stop the behavior and report it through the site's safety process.

**Active recall:** Which deliberate actions can turn a controlled task into a hazard?

#### 1.2.6 Unsafe Acts _(pp. 41–42)_

**Online-source summary:** Unsafe acts include using equipment incorrectly, ignoring controls, entering restricted areas, or working without required protection. Correct the action and the reason behind it rather than blaming only the worker.

**Active recall:** How is an unsafe act different from an unsafe condition?

#### 1.2.7 Rationalizing Risk _(p. 42)_

**Online-source summary:** Time pressure, convenience, confidence, and past success do not reduce a hazard's severity or likelihood. Reassess the task when the plan changes or a shortcut is proposed.

**Active recall:** What common excuses are used to rationalize unsafe work?

#### 1.2.8 Unsafe Conditions _(pp. 42–43)_

**Online-source summary:** Defective equipment, missing guards, poor access, uncontrolled energy, and unprotected edges are conditions that require correction or isolation. Do not begin or continue work where the required control is absent.

**Active recall:** What is your first response to an uncontrolled unsafe condition?

#### 1.2.9 Poor Housekeeping _(pp. 43–44)_

**Online-source summary:** Debris, cords, spills, blocked exits, and poorly stored materials create trip, fire, access, and struck-by hazards. Keep paths and emergency equipment clear and remove waste through the site's process.

**Active recall:** Which hazards can one blocked or cluttered walkway create?

#### 1.2.10 Management System Failure _(p. 44)_

**Online-source summary:** Incidents often expose failures in planning, training, supervision, maintenance, communication, or corrective action. A strong system looks beyond the last worker action to identify and control root causes.

**Active recall:** Why is “worker error” rarely a complete root-cause finding?

### 1.3.0 Hazard Recognition, Evaluation, and Control

#### 1.3.1 Hazard Recognition _(pp. 44–45)_

**Online-source summary:** Identify hazards before work and whenever people, equipment, materials, weather, or sequence changes. Review the site, task, manuals, SDSs, prior incidents, and worker input.

**Active recall:** Which changes should trigger a new hazard check?

#### 1.3.2 Job Safety Analysis (JSA) and Task Safety Analysis (TSA) _(p. 46)_

**Online-source summary:** A JSA or TSA breaks a job into steps, identifies the hazards at each step, and assigns controls before work starts. The analysis must reflect the actual task and be updated when conditions change.

**Active recall:** What are the three basic parts of a JSA or TSA?

#### 1.3.3 Risk Assessment _(pp. 46–48)_

**Online-source summary:** Risk assessment considers how severe an outcome could be and how likely exposure is, then prioritizes controls. Elimination and engineering controls are generally more reliable than depending only on behavior or PPE.

**Active recall:** What two factors are considered when prioritizing risk?

#### 1.3.4 Reporting Injuries, Incidents, and Near-Misses _(p. 48)_

**Online-source summary:** Report promptly through the employer's procedure so medical needs, scene control, investigation, and corrective action can begin. Preserve facts and avoid disturbing evidence unless needed to prevent further harm.

**Active recall:** What useful safety actions become possible after prompt reporting?

#### 1.3.5 Safety Data Sheets _(pp. 48–51)_

**Online-source summary:** An SDS communicates a chemical's hazards, protective measures, handling, storage, emergency, and first-aid information. Workers must know how to access the current SDS before using a hazardous chemical.

**Active recall:** Which SDS information should you check before handling a product?

## 2.0.0 Elevated Work and Fall Protection _(pp. 51–71)_

### 2.1.0 Fall Types and Hazards

#### 2.1.1 Walking and Working Surfaces _(p. 52)_

**Online-source summary:** Keep walking-working surfaces structurally sound, orderly, and free of avoidable slip and trip hazards. Inspect access and footing before carrying materials or beginning elevated work.

**Active recall:** What should be checked before stepping onto a work surface?

#### 2.1.2 Unprotected Sides, Wall Openings, and Floor Holes _(pp. 52–54)_

**Online-source summary:** Open edges and holes require the fall-protection system specified for the work and site. Never remove a cover or guardrail without authorization, replacement protection, and communication to affected workers.

**Active recall:** What controls can prevent a person or object from falling through an opening?

### 2.2.0 Fall Arrest

#### 2.2.1 Anchor Points _(p. 54)_

**Online-source summary:** A fall-arrest anchorage must be approved for the system and independent from unsuitable supports. Selection and installation belong to the employer and qualified-person process, not worker guesswork.

**Active recall:** Why is a convenient attachment point not automatically a safe anchorage?

#### 2.2.2 Harnesses _(pp. 55–58)_

**Online-source summary:** A full-body harness distributes arrest forces when selected, fitted, connected, and used as part of a complete system. Inspect it before use and remove damaged or involved-in-a-fall equipment from service as required.

**Active recall:** Why must harness fit and compatible connections both be checked?

#### 2.2.3 PFAS Inspection _(p. 58)_

**Online-source summary:** Inspect the entire personal fall arrest system for damaged webbing, stitching, hardware, labels, deformation, corrosion, contamination, and incompatible components. Follow manufacturer and employer criteria for removal from service.

**Active recall:** Which components of a PFAS must be inspected together?

#### 2.2.4 Lanyards _(pp. 58–59)_

**Online-source summary:** The lanyard must match the system, anchorage, connector, and required fall clearance. Protect it from sharp edges, heat, chemicals, and configurations that could increase free fall or prevent proper locking.

**Active recall:** Which site conditions can make a lanyard unsuitable?

#### 2.2.5 Lifelines _(pp. 59–61)_

**Online-source summary:** Vertical and horizontal lifelines require compatible connectors and an approved design, installation, and anchorage. Horizontal systems require qualified-person oversight because loads can be distributed in complex ways.

**Active recall:** Why does a horizontal lifeline need qualified design and supervision?

#### 2.2.6 Guardrails _(p. 61)_

**Online-source summary:** Guardrail systems prevent access to a fall edge when correctly built, maintained, and left in place. Report loose, incomplete, damaged, or temporarily removed rails immediately.

**Active recall:** What visible conditions can make a guardrail unreliable?

#### 2.2.7 Safety Nets _(pp. 61–62)_

**Online-source summary:** Safety nets are a designed fall-protection option that must be correctly installed, inspected, and kept clear of debris. Their location and configuration must limit fall distance and contact with lower hazards.

**Active recall:** What must remain clear beneath and inside a safety net?

### 2.3.0 Ladders and Stairs

#### 2.3.1 Straight Ladders _(pp. 62–63)_

**Online-source summary:** Select the correct ladder, place it on stable footing, secure it as required, maintain safe climbing contact, and keep the body positioned within the rails. Do not improvise a ladder or exceed its intended use.

**Active recall:** What conditions make a straight ladder unstable?

#### 2.3.2 Extension Ladders _(pp. 63–65)_

**Online-source summary:** Extension ladders require correct angle, overlap, support, access extension, and secure placement under the applicable procedure. Keep sections and locks engaged and stay clear of electrical and traffic hazards.

**Active recall:** What additional checks apply to an extension ladder?

#### 2.3.3 Stepladders _(pp. 65–67)_

**Online-source summary:** Fully open a stepladder on level support and lock its spreaders before use. Do not use it folded as a straight ladder or stand on prohibited steps identified by its label.

**Active recall:** Why must spreaders be fully opened and locked?

#### 2.3.4 Inspecting Ladders _(p. 67)_

**Online-source summary:** Check rails, rungs, feet, locks, ropes, hardware, labels, and contamination before use. Tag and remove a defective ladder from service rather than making an unauthorized repair.

**Active recall:** What should happen immediately after ladder damage is found?

#### 2.3.5 Stairways _(pp. 67–68)_

**Online-source summary:** Stairways need clear travel paths, secure treads, adequate lighting, and required rails or handrails. Do not store material on stairs or use incomplete access routes.

**Active recall:** Which conditions make a temporary stairway unsafe?

### 2.4.0 Scaffolds

#### 2.4.1 Types of Scaffolds _(p. 68)_

**Online-source summary:** Supported, suspended, and specialty scaffolds have different load, access, bracing, platform, and fall-protection requirements. Use only the type covered by your training and the competent person's direction.

**Active recall:** Why can rules for one scaffold type not be assumed for another?

#### 2.4.2 Inspecting Scaffolds _(pp. 68–70)_

**Online-source summary:** A competent person must inspect the scaffold and relevant components for visible defects before each shift and after events that could affect integrity. Workers should also report missing planks, guardrails, access, ties, bracing, or damaged parts.

**Active recall:** Which changes should trigger another scaffold inspection?

#### 2.4.3 Using Scaffolds _(pp. 70–71)_

**Online-source summary:** Use approved access, remain within load limits, keep platforms orderly, and follow the required fall and falling-object protection. Never move, alter, or climb scaffold components unless the system and procedure allow it.

**Active recall:** Which scaffold changes require competent-person control?

## 4.0.0 Energy Release Hazards _(pp. 80–86)_

### 4.1.0 Electrical Safety Guidelines

#### 4.1.1 Grounding _(pp. 80–81)_

**Online-source summary:** Grounding provides a path intended to limit hazardous voltage, but it does not make equipment automatically safe. Inspect cords and equipment, maintain grounding features, and use all required protective devices.

**Active recall:** Why is grounding only one part of electrical protection?

#### 4.1.2 Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters _(pp. 81–82)_

**Online-source summary:** A GFCI rapidly interrupts a circuit when it detects current imbalance that may indicate leakage to ground. Test and use it as required, while still controlling damaged cords, wet conditions, and contact hazards.

**Active recall:** Which hazards remain even when a GFCI is present?

#### 4.1.3 Summary of Electrical Safety Guidelines _(pp. 82–83)_

**Online-source summary:** De-energize whenever required, protect temporary wiring, inspect tools and cords, keep safe clearance, use required GFCI or grounding programs, and assign electrical work only to authorized qualified workers.

**Active recall:** Which controls should be checked before using temporary power?

#### 4.1.4 Working Near Energized Electrical Equipment _(pp. 83–84)_

**Online-source summary:** Treat exposed electrical parts as energized until an approved process proves otherwise. Maintain the required boundary, use barriers and qualified personnel, and do not approach or handle equipment beyond your authorization.

**Active recall:** What must be established before work near exposed energized parts begins?

#### 4.1.5 If Someone Is Shocked _(p. 84)_

**Online-source summary:** Do not touch a person who may still be in contact with electrical energy. Call emergency services, have the source safely de-energized by the appropriate person, and provide first aid or CPR only within your training when the scene is safe.

**Active recall:** Why must scene safety come before touching an electrical-shock victim?

### 4.2.0 Lockout/Tagout Requirements

**Online-source summary:** Lockout/tagout controls unexpected startup and the release of hazardous energy during servicing. Authorized workers identify every source, isolate and secure it, control stored energy, verify isolation, and follow the employer's restoration procedure.

**Active recall:** Why is switching equipment off not the same as energy isolation?

#### 4.2.1 Pressurized or High-Temperature Systems _(pp. 84–86)_

**Online-source summary:** Pressure and heat can remain after a machine is switched off. The energy-control procedure must isolate, block, bleed, cool, restrain, or otherwise control stored energy before work begins.

**Active recall:** Which forms of stored energy can remain after shutdown?

**Section sources:** [OSHA Electrical Focus Four guide](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/electr_ig.pdf) and [OSHA Control of Hazardous Energy](https://www.osha.gov/control-hazardous-energy)

## 5.0.0 Personal Protective Equipment _(pp. 86–97)_

### 5.1.0 PPE Items

**Online-source summary:** PPE reduces exposure when other controls cannot remove enough risk; it does not eliminate the hazard. Selection must follow the hazard assessment, fit the user, be compatible with other PPE, and be inspected and maintained.

**Active recall:** Why is PPE usually considered the last line of defense?

#### 5.1.1 Hard Hats _(pp. 87–88)_

**Online-source summary:** Head protection must match falling-object, impact, and electrical exposure and be worn and maintained as designed. Inspect the shell and suspension and replace equipment under employer and manufacturer criteria.

**Active recall:** Which hazards determine the required hard-hat type?

#### 5.1.2 Eye and Face Protection _(p. 88)_

**Online-source summary:** Match safety glasses, goggles, or face protection to impact, dust, splash, optical-radiation, and task hazards. A face shield normally supplements rather than automatically replaces primary eye protection.

**Active recall:** When might both eye and face protection be needed?

#### 5.1.3 Hand Protection _(pp. 89–90)_

**Online-source summary:** Select gloves for the actual cut, puncture, chemical, temperature, or electrical hazard while considering dexterity and entanglement. No single glove protects against every hazard.

**Active recall:** Why can the wrong glove increase risk around rotating equipment?

#### 5.1.4 Foot and Leg Protection _(pp. 90–91)_

**Online-source summary:** Foot and leg protection may address impact, compression, puncture, slip, chemical, heat, or electrical hazards. Fit, condition, sole, and task rating all matter.

**Active recall:** Which jobsite hazards should be considered when selecting footwear?

#### 5.1.5 Hearing Protection _(pp. 91–92)_

**Online-source summary:** Hearing protection is selected as part of a noise-control and hearing-conservation approach, not by comfort alone. Wear it correctly in designated areas and protect its seal and cleanliness.

**Active recall:** Why should loud noise be controlled at the source before relying only on earplugs?

### 5.2.0 Respiratory Hazards and Protection

#### 5.2.1 Silica Standard _(pp. 92–93)_

**Online-source summary:** Cutting, grinding, drilling, or disturbing silica-containing materials can create respirable dust. Follow the employer's exposure-control plan and required engineering, work-practice, access, housekeeping, and respiratory controls.

**Active recall:** Which common construction materials and tasks can create respirable silica?

#### 5.2.2 Other Respiratory Hazards _(pp. 93–95)_

**Online-source summary:** Dusts, fumes, mists, vapors, gases, fibers, and oxygen-deficient atmospheres require identification and exposure evaluation. Do not assume odor or visible dust reliably indicates danger.

**Active recall:** Why can an atmosphere be hazardous even when it looks and smells normal?

#### 5.2.3 Types of Respirators _(pp. 95–96)_

**Online-source summary:** Air-purifying and atmosphere-supplying respirators protect against different conditions and have important limitations. The employer's respiratory-protection program must select the approved type for the measured hazard.

**Active recall:** Why can an air-purifying respirator not be used for every atmosphere?

#### 5.2.4 Wearing a Respirator _(p. 96)_

**Online-source summary:** Required respirator use includes medical evaluation, training, fit testing for tight-fitting models, inspection, seal checks, cleaning, and correct storage. Facial hair or other interference can prevent a reliable seal.

**Active recall:** What program steps must happen before required respirator use?

#### 5.2.5 Selecting a Respirator _(pp. 96–97)_

**Online-source summary:** Selection depends on the contaminant, concentration, physical form, oxygen level, task, worksite, assigned protection factor, and worker factors. Workers should never select required protection by appearance alone.

**Active recall:** Which exposure information is needed before a respirator can be selected?

## 6.0.0 Job-Site Hazards _(pp. 97–117)_

### 6.1.0 Job-Site Exposure Hazards

#### 6.1.1 Lead _(pp. 97–98)_

**Online-source summary:** Lead can enter the body through inhalation or ingestion when lead-containing material is disturbed. Exposure assessment, hygiene, engineering controls, regulated practices, PPE, and medical requirements are employer responsibilities under the applicable standard.

**Online-source summary:** Treat blood and certain body fluids according to the exposure-control plan, avoid unprotected contact, and use the required barriers and disposal process. Report an exposure immediately so post-exposure procedures can begin.

**Active recall:** What should happen immediately after a possible exposure?

**Online-source summary:** Prevent splashes through substitution, closed handling, barriers, work practices, and chemical-compatible PPE. Know the SDS and location of emergency washing equipment before handling the chemical.

**Active recall:** Which information must be known before opening a hazardous chemical?

#### 6.1.4 Container Labeling _(pp. 99–100)_

**Online-source summary:** Hazard labels communicate the product identifier, signal word, pictograms, hazard statements, precautionary statements, and supplier information. Never use an unknown or improperly labeled chemical container.

**Active recall:** Which label elements connect the container to its SDS?

#### 6.1.5 Radiation Hazards _(pp. 100–101)_

**Online-source summary:** Ionizing and non-ionizing radiation sources require identification, controlled access, suitable shielding or distance, exposure limits, and task-specific protection. Only trained and authorized workers should enter controlled areas.

**Active recall:** Why must the radiation source and type be identified before selecting controls?

#### 6.1.6 Biological Hazards _(p. 101)_

**Online-source summary:** Mold, bacteria, viruses, insects, animals, sewage, and contaminated materials can create biological exposure. Use the site's assessment, hygiene, containment, PPE, and reporting procedures.

**Active recall:** Which biological hazards might be present during renovation or cleanup?

#### 6.1.7 Evacuation _(p. 101)_

**Online-source summary:** Know the alarm, assigned route, assembly location, reporting method, and accountability process before an emergency. Follow trained coordinators and do not re-enter until authorized.

### 6.2.0 Environmental Extremes

#### 6.2.1 Heat Stress _(p. 101)_

**Online-source summary:** Heat risk depends on environment, workload, clothing, acclimatization, health, hydration, and recovery. Prevention uses planning, water, rest, shade or cooling, training, monitoring, and prompt response to symptoms.

**Active recall:** Which personal and job factors can increase heat risk?

#### 6.2.2 Heat Cramps _(pp. 101–102)_

**Online-source summary:** Painful muscle cramps can result from salt and fluid loss during sweating. Stop strenuous work, move to a cooler area, follow the site's hydration and medical guidance, and report persistent symptoms.

**Active recall:** Why should heat cramps be treated as a warning rather than ignored?

#### 6.2.3 Heat Exhaustion _(p. 102)_

**Online-source summary:** Heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, thirst, or irritability can indicate heat exhaustion. Stop work, cool the worker, get assistance, and follow the emergency plan because the condition can worsen.

**Active recall:** Which signs distinguish heat exhaustion from ordinary fatigue?

#### 6.2.4 Heat Stroke _(pp. 102–103)_

**Online-source summary:** Confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures, or very high body temperature may indicate heat stroke, a life-threatening emergency. Call emergency services and begin the site's rapid cooling response immediately.

**Active recall:** Which mental-status changes make heat illness an emergency?

#### 6.2.5 Cold Stress _(p. 103)_

**Online-source summary:** Cold, wind, wet clothing, fatigue, and contact with cold surfaces can overwhelm the body's ability to stay warm. Use appropriate clothing, warm breaks, monitoring, and the buddy system.

**Active recall:** Why can wet conditions create cold stress above freezing?

#### 6.2.6 Frostbite _(p. 103)_

**Online-source summary:** Frostbite freezes skin and underlying tissue, commonly at extremities. Move the person to warmth, protect the area, seek medical help, and avoid rubbing or actions that could worsen tissue damage.

**Active recall:** Which body areas are especially vulnerable to frostbite?

#### 6.2.7 Hypothermia _(pp. 103–104)_

**Online-source summary:** Hypothermia occurs when heat loss exceeds heat production and can cause shivering, poor coordination, confusion, slowed breathing, and unconsciousness. Treat it as urgent, call for help, and follow trained rewarming procedures.

**Active recall:** Why can confusion be a dangerous sign of hypothermia?

#### 6.3.1 Arc Welding Hazards _(pp. 104–105)_

**Active recall:** Which hazards from arc welding can affect people beyond the welder?

#### 6.3.3 Transporting and Securing Cylinders _(pp. 106–107)_

**Active recall:** What could turn a damaged compressed-gas cylinder into a severe hazard?

#### 6.3.4 Hot Work Permits _(pp. 107–109)_

**Online-source summary:** A hot work permit confirms that hazards, combustibles, isolation, ventilation, fire watch, extinguishing equipment, and post-work monitoring have been addressed. It is a site authorization process, not merely paperwork.

**Active recall:** What conditions must be checked before hot work is authorized?

### 6.4.0 Fire Hazards and Fire Fighting

#### 6.4.1 How Fires Start _(p. 109)_

**Online-source summary:** Fire requires fuel, oxygen, heat, and a sustaining chemical reaction. Prevention works by separating or controlling one or more of these elements.

**Active recall:** Which fire element can the planned control remove or isolate?

#### 6.4.2 Combustibles _(pp. 109–110)_

**Online-source summary:** Identify combustible solids, liquids, gases, dusts, and hidden materials before ignition-producing work. Control quantities, storage, separation, housekeeping, and ignition sources.

**Active recall:** Which less-obvious combustibles might be hidden near hot work?

#### 6.4.3 Fire Prevention _(pp. 110–112)_

**Online-source summary:** Fire prevention combines housekeeping, approved storage, equipment maintenance, ignition control, permits, fire watch, alarms, access, and emergency planning. Maintain exits and firefighting equipment unobstructed.

**Active recall:** Which routine housekeeping actions also prevent fires?

#### 6.4.4 Basic Fire Fighting _(p. 112)_

**Online-source summary:** Workers should attempt incipient-stage firefighting only when employer policy authorizes it, they are trained, the extinguisher matches the fire, an exit remains available, and emergency reporting has begun. Otherwise, evacuate.

**Active recall:** Which conditions must all be true before a worker considers using an extinguisher?

#### 6.4.5 Using a Fire Extinguisher _(pp. 112–113)_

**Online-source summary:** Select only the extinguisher class specified for the fire and use it within your training while keeping a clear escape route. Stop and evacuate if the fire grows, smoke increases, the agent is ineffective, or safety becomes uncertain.

**Active recall:** What warning signs mean you should stop and evacuate?

### 6.5.0 Confined Spaces _(pp. 113–116)_

**Online-source summary:** A confined space has limited entry or exit, is large enough to enter, and is not designed for continuous occupancy; a permit space also contains or may contain serious hazards. ## Key vocabulary

**competent person**<br />
A person capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards and authorized to take prompt corrective measures, as defined for the applicable OSHA rule.

**qualified person**<br />
A person whose recognized knowledge, training, or experience meets the requirements of the specific technical task or standard.

**near-miss**<br />
An unplanned event that caused no injury or damage but had the potential to do so.

**JSA / TSA**<br />
A structured analysis that divides work into steps, identifies hazards, and assigns controls.

**PFAS**<br />
A personal fall arrest system: compatible components intended to safely stop a fall when properly designed and used.

**lockout/tagout**<br />
An energy-control process used to prevent unexpected startup or hazardous energy release during servicing.

**SDS**<br />
A standardized Safety Data Sheet describing a chemical's hazards, protective measures, and emergency information.

**permit-required confined space**<br />
A confined space with a hazardous atmosphere, engulfment potential, trapping configuration, or another recognized serious hazard.
