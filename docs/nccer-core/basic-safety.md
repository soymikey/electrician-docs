# Module 00101: Basic Safety

This page organizes Module 00101 into 82 active-recall cards. Answer each **Front** question before expanding the **Back** to check your response.

:::danger[Safety boundary]
These study cards do not replace employer procedures, OSHA requirements, site-specific training, PPE selection, equipment instructions, emergency services, or direction from a competent or qualified person. Stop work and ask for supervision whenever a hazard or procedure is unclear.
:::

:::info[How to review]
Mark a card **Know** if you can explain the hazard, control, and required action without looking. Mark it **Unsure** if you remember only part of the answer, and **Do not know** if you cannot answer. Repeat the **Unsure** and **Do not know** cards first.
:::

:::caution[Course scope]
These cards summarize the supplied NCCER Core Module 00101 material. Safety rules can depend on the task, equipment, employer program, and applicable standard.
:::

## Section 1: Safety and Hazard Recognition

### 1.1.0 Incidents and Accidents

### 1.1.1 Incident and Accident Categories

**Front:** Why should a near-miss be reported even when nobody was hurt?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Injuries, illnesses, property damage, environmental releases, and near-misses are different outcomes, but all can reveal uncontrolled hazards. Near-misses should be investigated before the same conditions produce harm.

</details>

### 1.1.2 Costs

**Front:** Name two direct and two indirect incident costs.

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** An incident can create direct costs such as treatment and repairs and indirect costs such as delay, investigation, retraining, lost productivity, and damaged trust. Prevention protects people first and also avoids disruption.

</details>

### 1.2.0 Incident and Accident Causes

### 1.2.1 Failure to Communicate

**Front:** What should you do when a safety instruction is unclear?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Missing, unclear, or unconfirmed information can leave workers unaware of hazards or changes. Use the site's communication method, confirm critical instructions, and speak up when something is not understood.

</details>

### 1.2.2 At-Risk Work Habits

**Front:** Why does “nothing happened last time” fail as a safety argument?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Repeating shortcuts can make unsafe behavior feel normal even though the hazard remains. Pause, follow the approved procedure, and correct the condition instead of relying on past luck.

</details>

### 1.2.3 Alcohol and Drug Abuse

**Front:** Which abilities needed for construction work can impairment affect?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Impairment can reduce judgment, coordination, reaction time, and communication. A worker who may be impaired should not perform safety-sensitive work and must follow employer policy.

</details>

### 1.2.4 Lack of Skill

**Front:** What should you verify before accepting an unfamiliar task?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Training, authorization, and demonstrated competence must match the task. Do not operate equipment or perform specialized work merely because the task looks familiar.

</details>

### 1.2.5 Intentional Acts

**Front:** Which deliberate actions can turn a controlled task into a hazard?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Horseplay, bypassing controls, damaging safeguards, and deliberate rule violations create foreseeable risk. Stop the behavior and report it through the site's safety process.

</details>

### 1.2.6 Unsafe Acts

**Front:** How is an unsafe act different from an unsafe condition?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Unsafe acts include using equipment incorrectly, ignoring controls, entering restricted areas, or working without required protection. Correct the action and the reason behind it rather than blaming only the worker.

</details>

### 1.2.7 Rationalizing Risk

**Front:** What common excuses are used to rationalize unsafe work?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Time pressure, convenience, confidence, and past success do not reduce a hazard's severity or likelihood. Reassess the task when the plan changes or a shortcut is proposed.

</details>

### 1.2.8 Unsafe Conditions

**Front:** What is your first response to an uncontrolled unsafe condition?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Defective equipment, missing guards, poor access, uncontrolled energy, and unprotected edges are conditions that require correction or isolation. Do not begin or continue work where the required control is absent.

</details>

### 1.2.9 Poor Housekeeping

**Front:** Which hazards can one blocked or cluttered walkway create?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Debris, cords, spills, blocked exits, and poorly stored materials create trip, fire, access, and struck-by hazards. Keep paths and emergency equipment clear and remove waste through the site's process.

</details>

### 1.2.10 Management System Failure

**Front:** Why is “worker error” rarely a complete root-cause finding?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Incidents often expose failures in planning, training, supervision, maintenance, communication, or corrective action. A strong system looks beyond the last worker action to identify and control root causes.

</details>

### 1.3.0 Hazard Recognition, Evaluation, and Control

### 1.3.1 Hazard Recognition

**Front:** Which changes should trigger a new hazard check?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Identify hazards before work and whenever people, equipment, materials, weather, or sequence changes. Review the site, task, manuals, SDSs, prior incidents, and worker input.

</details>

### 1.3.2 Job Safety Analysis (JSA) and Task Safety Analysis (TSA)

**Front:** What are the three basic parts of a JSA or TSA?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A JSA or TSA breaks a job into steps, identifies the hazards at each step, and assigns controls before work starts. The analysis must reflect the actual task and be updated when conditions change.

</details>

### 1.3.3 Risk Assessment

**Front:** What two factors are considered when prioritizing risk?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Risk assessment considers how severe an outcome could be and how likely exposure is, then prioritizes controls. Elimination and engineering controls are generally more reliable than depending only on behavior or PPE.

</details>

### 1.3.4 Reporting Injuries, Incidents, and Near-Misses

**Front:** What useful safety actions become possible after prompt reporting?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Report promptly through the employer's procedure so medical needs, scene control, investigation, and corrective action can begin. Preserve facts and avoid disturbing evidence unless needed to prevent further harm.

</details>

### 1.3.5 Safety Data Sheets

**Front:** Which SDS information should you check before handling a product?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** An SDS communicates a chemical's hazards, protective measures, handling, storage, emergency, and first-aid information. Workers must know how to access the current SDS before using a hazardous chemical.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Given a changed task, identify the hazards, assess the risk, choose controls, communicate them, and explain what must be reported.
:::

## Section 2: Elevated Work and Fall Protection

### 2.1.0 Fall Types and Hazards

### 2.1.1 Walking and Working Surfaces

**Front:** What should be checked before stepping onto a work surface?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Keep walking-working surfaces structurally sound, orderly, and free of avoidable slip and trip hazards. Inspect access and footing before carrying materials or beginning elevated work.

</details>

### 2.1.2 Unprotected Sides, Wall Openings, and Floor Holes

**Front:** What controls can prevent a person or object from falling through an opening?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Open edges and holes require the fall-protection system specified for the work and site. Never remove a cover or guardrail without authorization, replacement protection, and communication to affected workers.

</details>

### 2.2.0 Fall Arrest

### 2.2.1 Anchor Points

**Front:** Why is a convenient attachment point not automatically a safe anchorage?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A fall-arrest anchorage must be approved for the system and independent from unsuitable supports. Selection and installation belong to the employer and qualified-person process, not worker guesswork.

</details>

### 2.2.2 Harnesses

**Front:** Why must harness fit and compatible connections both be checked?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A full-body harness distributes arrest forces when selected, fitted, connected, and used as part of a complete system. Inspect it before use and remove damaged or involved-in-a-fall equipment from service as required.

</details>

### 2.2.3 PFAS Inspection

**Front:** Which components of a PFAS must be inspected together?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Inspect the entire personal fall arrest system for damaged webbing, stitching, hardware, labels, deformation, corrosion, contamination, and incompatible components. Follow manufacturer and employer criteria for removal from service.

</details>

### 2.2.4 Lanyards

**Front:** Which site conditions can make a lanyard unsuitable?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** The lanyard must match the system, anchorage, connector, and required fall clearance. Protect it from sharp edges, heat, chemicals, and configurations that could increase free fall or prevent proper locking.

</details>

### 2.2.5 Lifelines

**Front:** Why does a horizontal lifeline need qualified design and supervision?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Vertical and horizontal lifelines require compatible connectors and an approved design, installation, and anchorage. Horizontal systems require qualified-person oversight because loads can be distributed in complex ways.

</details>

### 2.2.6 Guardrails

**Front:** What visible conditions can make a guardrail unreliable?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Guardrail systems prevent access to a fall edge when correctly built, maintained, and left in place. Report loose, incomplete, damaged, or temporarily removed rails immediately.

</details>

### 2.2.7 Safety Nets

**Front:** What must remain clear beneath and inside a safety net?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Safety nets are a designed fall-protection option that must be correctly installed, inspected, and kept clear of debris. Their location and configuration must limit fall distance and contact with lower hazards.

</details>

### 2.3.0 Ladders and Stairs

### 2.3.1 Straight Ladders

**Front:** What conditions make a straight ladder unstable?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Select the correct ladder, place it on stable footing, secure it as required, maintain safe climbing contact, and keep the body positioned within the rails. Do not improvise a ladder or exceed its intended use.

</details>

### 2.3.2 Extension Ladders

**Front:** What additional checks apply to an extension ladder?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Extension ladders require correct angle, overlap, support, access extension, and secure placement under the applicable procedure. Keep sections and locks engaged and stay clear of electrical and traffic hazards.

</details>

### 2.3.3 Stepladders

**Front:** Why must spreaders be fully opened and locked?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Fully open a stepladder on level support and lock its spreaders before use. Do not use it folded as a straight ladder or stand on prohibited steps identified by its label.

</details>

### 2.3.4 Inspecting Ladders

**Front:** What should happen immediately after ladder damage is found?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Check rails, rungs, feet, locks, ropes, hardware, labels, and contamination before use. Tag and remove a defective ladder from service rather than making an unauthorized repair.

</details>

### 2.3.5 Stairways

**Front:** Which conditions make a temporary stairway unsafe?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Stairways need clear travel paths, secure treads, adequate lighting, and required rails or handrails. Do not store material on stairs or use incomplete access routes.

</details>

### 2.4.0 Scaffolds

### 2.4.1 Types of Scaffolds

**Front:** Why can rules for one scaffold type not be assumed for another?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Supported, suspended, and specialty scaffolds have different load, access, bracing, platform, and fall-protection requirements. Use only the type covered by your training and the competent person's direction.

</details>

### 2.4.2 Inspecting Scaffolds

**Front:** Which changes should trigger another scaffold inspection?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A competent person must inspect the scaffold and relevant components for visible defects before each shift and after events that could affect integrity. Workers should also report missing planks, guardrails, access, ties, bracing, or damaged parts.

</details>

### 2.4.3 Using Scaffolds

**Front:** Which scaffold changes require competent-person control?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Use approved access, remain within load limits, keep platforms orderly, and follow the required fall and falling-object protection. Never move, alter, or climb scaffold components unless the system and procedure allow it.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Choose the correct access and fall-protection approach only after the surface, exposure, equipment, and site procedure have been evaluated.
:::

## Section 3: Struck-By and Caught-in-Between Hazards

### 3.1.0 Struck-By Hazards

### 3.1.1 Falling Objects

**Front:** Which controls protect people below overhead work?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Control falling-object hazards with secure storage, toe boards or barriers, exclusion zones, tool retention where appropriate, and head protection. Never work or pass beneath a suspended or unsecured load.

</details>

### 3.1.2 Flying Objects

**Front:** Why may safety glasses alone be insufficient for some flying-object tasks?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Cutting, grinding, fastening, and pressurized operations can launch fragments or tools. Use the machine guard, correct eye and face protection, and a controlled work zone.

</details>

### 3.1.3 Vehicle and Roadway Hazards

**Front:** Where are the common blind and struck-by zones around equipment?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Separate people and mobile equipment with traffic plans, barriers, visibility, signals, and designated routes. Establish communication with operators and stay out of blind spots and swing areas.

</details>

### 3.2.0 Caught-In and Caught-Between Hazards

### 3.2.1 Trenches and Excavations

**Front:** Which hazards besides cave-in must be evaluated in an excavation?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Cave-in, hazardous atmosphere, water, utilities, falling material, and access hazards require competent-person evaluation and the specified protective system. Never enter an excavation that has not been inspected and released for entry.

</details>

### 3.2.2 Tool, Machine, and Equipment Guards

**Front:** What should you do if a guard interferes with the planned task?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Guards prevent contact with moving parts, nip points, and ejected material. Do not remove, bypass, or operate around a missing guard; isolate the equipment and report the defect.

</details>

### 3.2.3 Cranes and Heavy Equipment

**Front:** Which areas around a crane or machine can trap or crush a worker?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Stay clear of suspended loads, counterweight swing radii, pinch points, and travel paths. Follow the lift or traffic plan and communicate only through the site's designated signaling system.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
For any moving object or equipment, identify what can fall, fly, swing, roll, collapse, pull in, or pin a worker.
:::

## Section 4: Energy Release Hazards

### 4.1.0 Electrical Safety Guidelines

### 4.1.1 Grounding

**Front:** Why is grounding only one part of electrical protection?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Grounding provides a path intended to limit hazardous voltage, but it does not make equipment automatically safe. Inspect cords and equipment, maintain grounding features, and use all required protective devices.

</details>

### 4.1.2 Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters

**Front:** Which hazards remain even when a GFCI is present?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A GFCI rapidly interrupts a circuit when it detects current imbalance that may indicate leakage to ground. Test and use it as required, while still controlling damaged cords, wet conditions, and contact hazards.

</details>

### 4.1.3 Summary of Electrical Safety Guidelines

**Front:** Which controls should be checked before using temporary power?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** De-energize whenever required, protect temporary wiring, inspect tools and cords, keep safe clearance, use required GFCI or grounding programs, and assign electrical work only to authorized qualified workers.

</details>

### 4.1.4 Working Near Energized Electrical Equipment

**Front:** What must be established before work near exposed energized parts begins?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Treat exposed electrical parts as energized until an approved process proves otherwise. Maintain the required boundary, use barriers and qualified personnel, and do not approach or handle equipment beyond your authorization.

</details>

### 4.1.5 If Someone Is Shocked

**Front:** Why must scene safety come before touching an electrical-shock victim?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Do not touch a person who may still be in contact with electrical energy. Call emergency services, have the source safely de-energized by the appropriate person, and provide first aid or CPR only within your training when the scene is safe.

</details>

### 4.2.0 Lockout/Tagout Requirements

**Front:** Why is switching equipment off not the same as energy isolation?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Lockout/tagout controls unexpected startup and the release of hazardous energy during servicing. Authorized workers identify every source, isolate and secure it, control stored energy, verify isolation, and follow the employer's restoration procedure.

</details>

### 4.2.1 Pressurized or High-Temperature Systems

**Front:** Which forms of stored energy can remain after shutdown?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Pressure and heat can remain after a machine is switched off. The energy-control procedure must isolate, block, bleed, cool, restrain, or otherwise control stored energy before work begins.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Identify every energy source, including stored and secondary energy, then explain who is authorized to isolate and verify it.
:::

## Section 5: Personal Protective Equipment

### 5.1.0 PPE Items

**Front:** Why is PPE usually considered the last line of defense?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** PPE reduces exposure when other controls cannot remove enough risk; it does not eliminate the hazard. Selection must follow the hazard assessment, fit the user, be compatible with other PPE, and be inspected and maintained.

</details>

### 5.1.1 Hard Hats

**Front:** Which hazards determine the required hard-hat type?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Head protection must match falling-object, impact, and electrical exposure and be worn and maintained as designed. Inspect the shell and suspension and replace equipment under employer and manufacturer criteria.

</details>

### 5.1.2 Eye and Face Protection

**Front:** When might both eye and face protection be needed?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Match safety glasses, goggles, or face protection to impact, dust, splash, optical-radiation, and task hazards. A face shield normally supplements rather than automatically replaces primary eye protection.

</details>

### 5.1.3 Hand Protection

**Front:** Why can the wrong glove increase risk around rotating equipment?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Select gloves for the actual cut, puncture, chemical, temperature, or electrical hazard while considering dexterity and entanglement. No single glove protects against every hazard.

</details>

### 5.1.4 Foot and Leg Protection

**Front:** Which jobsite hazards should be considered when selecting footwear?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Foot and leg protection may address impact, compression, puncture, slip, chemical, heat, or electrical hazards. Fit, condition, sole, and task rating all matter.

</details>

### 5.1.5 Hearing Protection

**Front:** Why should loud noise be controlled at the source before relying only on earplugs?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Hearing protection is selected as part of a noise-control and hearing-conservation approach, not by comfort alone. Wear it correctly in designated areas and protect its seal and cleanliness.

</details>

### 5.2.0 Respiratory Hazards and Protection

### 5.2.1 Silica Standard

**Front:** Which common construction materials and tasks can create respirable silica?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Cutting, grinding, drilling, or disturbing silica-containing materials can create respirable dust. Follow the employer's exposure-control plan and required engineering, work-practice, access, housekeeping, and respiratory controls.

</details>

### 5.2.2 Other Respiratory Hazards

**Front:** Why can an atmosphere be hazardous even when it looks and smells normal?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Dusts, fumes, mists, vapors, gases, fibers, and oxygen-deficient atmospheres require identification and exposure evaluation. Do not assume odor or visible dust reliably indicates danger.

</details>

### 5.2.3 Types of Respirators

**Front:** Why can an air-purifying respirator not be used for every atmosphere?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Air-purifying and atmosphere-supplying respirators protect against different conditions and have important limitations. The employer's respiratory-protection program must select the approved type for the measured hazard.

</details>

### 5.2.4 Wearing a Respirator

**Front:** What program steps must happen before required respirator use?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Required respirator use includes medical evaluation, training, fit testing for tight-fitting models, inspection, seal checks, cleaning, and correct storage. Facial hair or other interference can prevent a reliable seal.

</details>

### 5.2.5 Selecting a Respirator

**Front:** Which exposure information is needed before a respirator can be selected?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Selection depends on the contaminant, concentration, physical form, oxygen level, task, worksite, assigned protection factor, and worker factors. Workers should never select required protection by appearance alone.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
For each hazard, explain the higher-level controls first, then the PPE type, fit, compatibility, inspection, and training requirements.
:::

## Section 6: Job-Site Hazards

### 6.1.0 Job-Site Exposure Hazards

### 6.1.1 Lead

**Front:** How can lead move from a work task into a worker's body or home?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Lead can enter the body through inhalation or ingestion when lead-containing material is disturbed. Exposure assessment, hygiene, engineering controls, regulated practices, PPE, and medical requirements are employer responsibilities under the applicable standard.

</details>

### 6.1.2 Bloodborne Pathogens

**Front:** What should happen immediately after a possible exposure?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Treat blood and certain body fluids according to the exposure-control plan, avoid unprotected contact, and use the required barriers and disposal process. Report an exposure immediately so post-exposure procedures can begin.

</details>

### 6.1.3 Chemical Splashes

**Front:** Which information must be known before opening a hazardous chemical?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Prevent splashes through substitution, closed handling, barriers, work practices, and chemical-compatible PPE. Know the SDS and location of emergency washing equipment before handling the chemical.

</details>

### 6.1.4 Container Labeling

**Front:** Which label elements connect the container to its SDS?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Hazard labels communicate the product identifier, signal word, pictograms, hazard statements, precautionary statements, and supplier information. Never use an unknown or improperly labeled chemical container.

</details>

### 6.1.5 Radiation Hazards

**Front:** Why must the radiation source and type be identified before selecting controls?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Ionizing and non-ionizing radiation sources require identification, controlled access, suitable shielding or distance, exposure limits, and task-specific protection. Only trained and authorized workers should enter controlled areas.

</details>

### 6.1.6 Biological Hazards

**Front:** Which biological hazards might be present during renovation or cleanup?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Mold, bacteria, viruses, insects, animals, sewage, and contaminated materials can create biological exposure. Use the site's assessment, hygiene, containment, PPE, and reporting procedures.

</details>

### 6.1.7 Evacuation

**Front:** Where do you report after leaving the work area?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Know the alarm, assigned route, assembly location, reporting method, and accountability process before an emergency. Follow trained coordinators and do not re-enter until authorized.

</details>

### 6.2.0 Environmental Extremes

### 6.2.1 Heat Stress

**Front:** Which personal and job factors can increase heat risk?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Heat risk depends on environment, workload, clothing, acclimatization, health, hydration, and recovery. Prevention uses planning, water, rest, shade or cooling, training, monitoring, and prompt response to symptoms.

</details>

### 6.2.2 Heat Cramps

**Front:** Why should heat cramps be treated as a warning rather than ignored?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Painful muscle cramps can result from salt and fluid loss during sweating. Stop strenuous work, move to a cooler area, follow the site's hydration and medical guidance, and report persistent symptoms.

</details>

### 6.2.3 Heat Exhaustion

**Front:** Which signs distinguish heat exhaustion from ordinary fatigue?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, thirst, or irritability can indicate heat exhaustion. Stop work, cool the worker, get assistance, and follow the emergency plan because the condition can worsen.

</details>

### 6.2.4 Heat Stroke

**Front:** Which mental-status changes make heat illness an emergency?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures, or very high body temperature may indicate heat stroke, a life-threatening emergency. Call emergency services and begin the site's rapid cooling response immediately.

</details>

### 6.2.5 Cold Stress

**Front:** Why can wet conditions create cold stress above freezing?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Cold, wind, wet clothing, fatigue, and contact with cold surfaces can overwhelm the body's ability to stay warm. Use appropriate clothing, warm breaks, monitoring, and the buddy system.

</details>

### 6.2.6 Frostbite

**Front:** Which body areas are especially vulnerable to frostbite?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Frostbite freezes skin and underlying tissue, commonly at extremities. Move the person to warmth, protect the area, seek medical help, and avoid rubbing or actions that could worsen tissue damage.

</details>

### 6.2.7 Hypothermia

**Front:** Why can confusion be a dangerous sign of hypothermia?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Hypothermia occurs when heat loss exceeds heat production and can cause shivering, poor coordination, confusion, slowed breathing, and unconsciousness. Treat it as urgent, call for help, and follow trained rewarming procedures.

</details>

### 6.3.0 Hot Work Hazards

### 6.3.1 Arc Welding Hazards

**Front:** Which hazards from arc welding can affect people beyond the welder?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Arc welding can expose workers to electric shock, intense light, burns, fumes, fire, and nearby-worker exposure. Controls include trained setup, ventilation, screens, grounding, PPE, and fire prevention.

</details>

### 6.3.2 Oxyfuel Cutting, Welding, and Brazing

**Front:** Why must oxygen equipment be kept free from oil and contamination?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Fuel gas and oxygen systems add leak, fire, explosion, flashback, fume, and burn hazards. Inspect and use only compatible equipment under the approved procedure and keep ignition and combustibles controlled.

</details>

### 6.3.3 Transporting and Securing Cylinders

**Front:** What could turn a damaged compressed-gas cylinder into a severe hazard?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Protect cylinders and valves from damage, secure cylinders upright as required, use approved handling equipment, and separate or store gases according to the procedure. Never use a valve cap as a lifting point.

</details>

### 6.3.4 Hot Work Permits

**Front:** What conditions must be checked before hot work is authorized?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A hot work permit confirms that hazards, combustibles, isolation, ventilation, fire watch, extinguishing equipment, and post-work monitoring have been addressed. It is a site authorization process, not merely paperwork.

</details>

### 6.4.0 Fire Hazards and Fire Fighting

### 6.4.1 How Fires Start

**Front:** Which fire element can the planned control remove or isolate?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Fire requires fuel, oxygen, heat, and a sustaining chemical reaction. Prevention works by separating or controlling one or more of these elements.

</details>

### 6.4.2 Combustibles

**Front:** Which less-obvious combustibles might be hidden near hot work?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Identify combustible solids, liquids, gases, dusts, and hidden materials before ignition-producing work. Control quantities, storage, separation, housekeeping, and ignition sources.

</details>

### 6.4.3 Fire Prevention

**Front:** Which routine housekeeping actions also prevent fires?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Fire prevention combines housekeeping, approved storage, equipment maintenance, ignition control, permits, fire watch, alarms, access, and emergency planning. Maintain exits and firefighting equipment unobstructed.

</details>

### 6.4.4 Basic Fire Fighting

**Front:** Which conditions must all be true before a worker considers using an extinguisher?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Workers should attempt incipient-stage firefighting only when employer policy authorizes it, they are trained, the extinguisher matches the fire, an exit remains available, and emergency reporting has begun. Otherwise, evacuate.

</details>

### 6.4.5 Using a Fire Extinguisher

**Front:** What warning signs mean you should stop and evacuate?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Select only the extinguisher class specified for the fire and use it within your training while keeping a clear escape route. Stop and evacuate if the fire grows, smoke increases, the agent is ineffective, or safety becomes uncertain.

</details>

### 6.5.0 Confined Spaces

**Front:** What additional hazards can make a confined space permit-required?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A confined space has limited entry or exit, is large enough to enter, and is not designed for continuous occupancy; a permit space also contains or may contain serious hazards. Only assigned and trained workers may enter after evaluation, isolation, testing, permit controls, attendant and rescue provisions required by the program are in place.

</details>
