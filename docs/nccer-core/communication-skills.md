# Module 00107: Basic Communication Skills

This page organizes Module 00107 into 16 active-recall cards. Answer each **Front** question before expanding the **Back** to check your response.

## Section 1: Communication

### 1.0.0 Communication

**Front:** What is two-way communication, and what can interfere with it?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** A sender delivers a spoken or written message through a channel, the receiver interprets it, and the receiver gives feedback or asks questions to confirm understanding. Interference, called **noise**, includes unclear speech, jargon, disorganized writing, distractions, poor listening, actual jobsite noise, and equipment problems.

</details>

### 1.1.1 Nonverbal Communication

**Front:** What forms of nonverbal communication affect how others interpret you?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Grooming, dress, the condition of your work area, use of time, facial expressions, eye contact, posture, gestures, and physical distance all send messages. They can communicate professionalism, interest, confidence, respect, boredom, or carelessness even when no words are spoken.

</details>

### 1.1.2 Listening and Speaking Skills

**Front:** Why are listening and speaking considered construction tools?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Workers use them to give and receive instructions, participate in presentations and team discussions, and communicate with coworkers, supervisors, and clients. Clear speaking and accurate listening directly affect safety, quality, schedules, and cost.

</details>

### Active Listening

**Front:** What habits make listening active rather than passive?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Remove distractions, face the speaker, maintain appropriate eye contact, do not interrupt, ask questions, and take notes when details include numbers, times, amounts, or temperatures. Finish by paraphrasing the message in your own words so the speaker can confirm or correct your understanding.

</details>

### 1.2.1 Barriers to Listening

**Front:** What common barriers prevent effective listening, and how can you overcome them?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Emotion, boredom, distractions, and ego can block a message. Calm down before continuing, force yourself to focus, move away from noise when possible, explain serious concentration problems to a supervisor, and stop planning your reply while the other person is still speaking.

</details>

### Speaking on the Job

**Front:** What practices make jobsite instructions clear and effective?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Organize the message logically, use specific words, select a quiet time and place, explain unfamiliar jargon, speak clearly, maintain appropriate eye contact, and allow questions. Introduce the topic, give the details, summarize the message, and confirm that everyone understands.

</details>

### 1.3.1 Placing Telephone Calls

**Front:** How should you place a professional telephone call or leave a message?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Identify yourself, confirm whom you are speaking with, clearly state the purpose, and take notes. When leaving a message, plan it, keep it brief, and provide your callback number and the best time to reach you. Never use a phone while driving, operating machinery, or using a power tool.

</details>

### 1.3.2 Receiving Telephone Calls

**Front:** How should you receive and manage a professional telephone call?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Answer courteously with your name and company, avoid leaving callers on hold for long, transfer calls politely, introduce the caller to the recipient, and keep the call brief. Personal calls should wait until a break so they do not interfere with work or create a safety hazard.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Extending a meeting past its scheduled time does **not** show respect, so the statement is False. Take notes when technical information includes data. Give assembly instructions in a quiet place when workers are alert. Cell phones may be banned because they can cause accidents, waste time, and expose intellectual property. Begin a call by identifying yourself.
:::

## Section 2: Reading and Writing

### 2.0.0 Reading and Writing

**Front:** How do reading and writing affect a construction professional's work and advancement?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Reading is necessary for codes, safety rules, plans, specifications, manufacturer instructions, contracts, and work documents. Writing is used for work orders, reports, punch lists, permits, material takeoffs, change orders, memos, emails, and texts. Strong skills improve safety and accuracy and prepare workers for greater responsibility.

</details>

### 2.1.0 The Importance of Reading and Writing Skills

**Front:** Why should a worker read job documents directly instead of relying only on someone else's explanation?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Codes, safety guidance, specifications, and manufacturer instructions may contain exact requirements that affect safe and correct performance. Reading them directly reduces lost details, supports independent decisions, and helps the worker recognize changes that apply to the job.

</details>

### Finding Information in Technical Material

**Front:** When should you use a table of contents, index, glossary, or appendix?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Use the **table of contents** to see the document's overall organization, the **index** to locate pages about a specific topic, the **glossary** to find definitions, and an **appendix** for detailed supporting information placed outside the main text.

</details>

### Reading to Understand

**Front:** Which techniques improve comprehension and memory when reading technical instructions?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Reduce distractions, preview headings, notice bold or italic text, take notes or highlight key information, and record useful page numbers. Read all steps before starting, picture yourself performing the task, reread difficult material, and take a short break when fatigue reduces concentration.

</details>

### The Writing Process

**Front:** What are the major steps in the writing process?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Prewrite by brainstorming, organizing, outlining, and researching; write a rough draft; set it aside so you can return with a fresh view; revise the content and organization; then proofread grammar, spelling, numbers, and word choice. A coworker can provide a final accuracy check.

</details>

### Characteristics of Good Writing

**Front:** What five characteristics should effective workplace writing have?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Good writing is **clear, concise, correct, complete, and considerate** of the reader. It identifies the purpose and needed action, includes who, what, when, where, why, and how as appropriate, uses accurate details, removes unnecessary words, and avoids unexplained technical language.

</details>

### 2.3.1 Emails

**Front:** What rules make a business email professional and safe?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Treat it like a formal letter: verify recipients, use a clear subject and greeting, keep the message brief, state the required action, use a positive tone, identify attachments, and proofread before sending. Do not use all caps, sarcasm, confidential information, bad news, or emotional attacks; call or meet when email is unsuitable.

</details>

### 2.3.2 Texting

**Front:** When is a work text appropriate, and what precautions are required?

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Back:** Texting suits brief, immediate information or confirmation that requires little interaction. Call or use another method for long, complex, emotional, apologetic, or critical messages. Follow company policy, use abbreviations only when the recipient understands them, and proofread for autocorrect errors. Never send texts while driving or operating machinery.

</details>

:::tip[Section Review focus]
Poor reading skills often cause workers to avoid technical information. Use an index to find whether a book discusses a particular topic. Bold type indicates important words. After a rough draft, rest before reviewing and revising it. Treat a business email like a formal business letter.
:::
